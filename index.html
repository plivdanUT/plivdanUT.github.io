About

Philip Livdan
plivdan@utexas.edu

I am a PhD student in finance at the McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin.

I study asset pricing with machine learning and the financing of capital that becomes obsolete quickly. My current papers ask what a neural network reads from a picture of past prices and trades, and how lenders and suppliers share the risk that new equipment makes old equipment worth less.

Image Encodings of Corporate Bond Trades

with Donal O'Cofaigh | Work in progress

Abstract

A stock has a standard picture, the daily price chart. A corporate bond does not. Many bonds go days without a trade, small trades print far from large trades on the same day, and the tape records which side the customer took. We draw the same 60-day window of TRACE trades in several formats and train one convolutional network on each format to predict the bond's return over Treasuries in the next month. In about 3,000 bonds from 2002 to 2025, formats that show only prices and volume, including the bar chart used for stocks, do not predict returns out of sample. A format that shows the time between trades does. Its decile long-short portfolio earns 0.23 percent a month from 2011 to 2025 (t = 3.6). Simple counts of how often a bond trades account for that return, so the image so far adds nothing to them.

What Explains Image-Based Returns?

Work in progress

Abstract

I train a variational autoencoder on 20-day price charts of US stocks from 1992 to 2025. The model sees only the images and never sees returns. A long-short portfolio formed on its 16 latent variables, with weights estimated through 2009, earns 7.2 percent a year equal-weighted out of sample from 2016 to 2025 (t = 2.7). Its alpha against the Fama-French five factors, momentum, and short-term and long-term reversal is 7.7 percent (t = 2.9). The value-weighted return is 2.2 percent (t = 0.5). Eleven named chart statistics, such as the past 20-day return, the slope of the moving average, and price acceleration, account for the equal-weighted return. Once I remove the part of the signal they explain, the portfolio earns 1.2 percent (t = 0.3).

Portfolio weights are estimated on 1992 to 2009. The vertical lines mark the start of the validation period in 2010 and of the test period in 2016.

Financing Obsolescence: Vendor Guarantees as Commitment When the Supplier Controls Depreciation

Work in progress

When a supplier releases a new product generation, the older equipment its customers pledged as collateral loses value, so lenders restrict financing in advance. A residual-value guarantee from the supplier insures lenders and also commits the supplier to slower releases, which outside insurance cannot do. The setting is the financing of data-center GPUs.
